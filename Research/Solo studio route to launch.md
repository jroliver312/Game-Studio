# Sovereign's route from Gate A to Steam

A one-person studio gets from finished design docs to a successful launch by running two tracks in parallel from now on. The first is a gated production track that, for a systems-heavy sim, proves the connected simulation before it polishes anything. The second is a market track that opens a Steam page 6–12 months before release and stacks its wishlist beats into the final four months. The studio's job is much wider than "develop and engineer". It covers roughly twenty functions, from capsule art and localization to tax verification, accessibility, creator outreach, crash telemetry and post-launch DLC. Successful solo sim makers keep the iteration-heavy work (design, simulation code, UX logic, QA, community) and buy bounded deliverables such as key art, music, trailer and translation. The market data is unforgiving. Wishlists explain only about half of first-month sales variance, the median Next Fest entrant gains under 500 wishlists, and roughly half of 2025's ~21k Steam releases ended the year with 10 or fewer reviews. But strategy, simulation and management sit among the genres that reliably outperform, and a $19.99–24.99 premium price places Sovereign in a cohort whose median gross is an order of magnitude above Steam's overall median. Political and management sims made by one to three people (Democracy, Ostriv, Shadow Empire, RimWorld, Dwarf Fortress) show the template: own the simulation, buy the art, support mods from day one, expect a multi-year timeline, and earn the long tail through updates and DLC. Two risks are specific to Sovereign. Real geography invites accuracy and neutrality fights, and political-sim ads get blocked on Meta. Both need policies before the store page goes live.

## The studio is twenty jobs, and only a few are code

Classic studio role lists include a producer (who in small shops also owns PR, contracts and beta management), a designer who often doubles as writer, 2D and 3D artists, programmers, a sound engineer and testers whose role grows near completion ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_development)). A Steam release adds functions that role lists omit: store-page craft, Steam Deck certification, accessibility, localization, build and release engineering, analytics, creator relations, legal and tax, live ops and the post-mortem. The working rule that emerges from both the outsourcing literature and solo post-mortems is to **keep what is iterative and systemic, and outsource what can be fully specified**. Pandemic's Carey Chico argued at GDC that outsourcing works only with precise content guides, fixed delivery formats and a one-revision-per-asset discipline, because "revision shouldn't be an iterative process" ([Game Developer, 2009](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/game-platforms/gdc-how-do-you-outsource-successfully-as-a-developer-)). Solo developer Charles Cordingley called hiring a 2D/GUI freelancer "one of his best decisions", and player feedback improved immediately. He also admitted he could not market and develop at the same time ([Game Developer, 2016](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/concealed-intent-development-postmortem)).

Summed from these floors, a lean outsourcing budget for Sovereign looks like this: capsule art $250–2k, a trailer $500–6k, about 20 minutes of music near $8k, and localization into five languages at 30k words roughly €13.5–18k. That is illustrative arithmetic on dated figures, not a quote. Localization is the largest and most deferrable line. Cordingley still advises planning for it early because many of his buyers were non-English speakers ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/concealed-intent-development-postmortem)). For a geography-based political sim, string externalization should be a pre-production engineering requirement even if translation is bought post-launch.

## Prove the connected simulation before polishing a slice

Standard milestone definitions are loose but consistent. First playable has representative gameplay. Alpha is feature-complete with all major systems connected. Beta is feature- and asset-complete with only bug fixing. Release candidate is a build that could ship ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Video_game_development)). A 2026 milestone guide adds the useful exit rules: scope lock at alpha, "new feature work should stop" at beta, and gold is "a verification stage, not a development stage" ([PC Tech Mag, 2026-06-23](https://pctechmag.com/2026/06/unity-production-milestones-what-each-stage-should-deliver/)). Clinton Keith's agile model fits a solo developer's rhythm. Concept and pre-production are iterative and aimed at knowledge rather than value. In production, velocity becomes the rate of content throughput ([CCSU notes on Keith](https://cs.ccsu.edu/~stan/classes/CS415/notes/06-AgileProjects.html)).

The critical adjustment for Sovereign is the order of slices. Borut Pfeifer argues that the vertical slice fails for simulation games, because "building 2-3 minutes of polished gameplay is largely impossible until the game is in post-production" when the experience emerges from system interaction ([Game Developer, 2013](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/building-simulations-part-4-the-connected-systems-playable)). His replacement is the **connected-systems playable**: every system present at the lowest possible fidelity, roughly 5–7 high-level systems, each with at least one input from and one output to another, with deliberate positive and negative feedback loops. It exists to reveal that "gameplay critical events will either happen too much, or too little" ([Pfeifer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/production/building-simulations-part-4-the-connected-systems-playable)). A deterministic core makes this milestone unusually strong, because seeded replays turn balance problems into reproducible regression tests. The polished vertical slice still matters, but as a marketing milestone: one region and one era at final quality, feeding the capsule, screenshots, trailer and Next Fest demo.

| Gate | Exit criteria | Sovereign artifact |
|---|---|---|
| A: foundation (pending) | Vision and hook on one page; risk list; test plan; core loop judged worth building | Existing design docs plus a frozen one-page pitch |
| B: connected-systems playable | All 5–7 systems linked at minimum fidelity; seeded runs reproducible; event frequencies tuned | Grey-box sim on one region/era, headless replay tests |
| C: marketing slice | Art direction, UI, controller nav, 1280×800 legibility locked; capsule and trailer footage exist | Store page live, demo candidate |
| D: alpha (feature lock) | All eras/regions supported structurally; mod/data format frozen; end-to-end QA begins | Steam Playtest build |
| E: beta (content lock) | No new features; ratings, privacy policy, crash reporting, AI disclosure signed off | Next Fest demo or EA build |
| F: RC and gold | Build passes Steam review; store assets and localized descriptions final | Release build; launch discount set |

The Game Outcomes Project explains why gates matter more than methodology. Across 272 projects, the type of process made no measurable difference. A shared, stable vision, realistic schedules and frequent feedback helped. Frequent changes to the core design, confused goals and crunch hurt most, and an early design document had little effect ([Skarupke summary](https://probablydance.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/evidence_for_how_to_make_great_games.pdf)). For a solo developer, "whole-team prioritization" becomes a written go/kill/rescope decision at each gate, and external playtesters stand in for teammates. Estimates need padding. Cordingley planned 18 months and shipped in about four years, after reworking every system ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/concealed-intent-development-postmortem)). For a part-time developer, that 2×+ multiplier is a floor.

Steam's own gates fix the end of the calendar, and some of them open early. The Steam Direct page states a **30-day gap between paying the fee and releasing for a developer's first few titles**, and identity, bank and tax verification takes **about 15–30 days** ([Steamworks Steam Direct](https://partner.steamgames.com/steamdirect)). Pay the fee and start verification well before the store page is wanted. Store-page review takes 3–5 business days (submit 7 ahead), build review another 3–5 (plan 7), and approved builds can then be updated freely ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/review_process)). A Coming Soon page must be live at least two weeks before release ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/store/coming_soon)). Steam Deck Verified adds a 30fps-at-800p floor, full controller access without changing settings, input-matched glyphs and no launcher friction ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/steamdeck/compat)). These overlap heavily with the GAG basic tier's remapping, readable text and colour-independence rules ([GAG](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)), so designing UI controller-first at Gate C makes both nearly free.

## Wishlists explain half the outcome; timing explains much of the rest

VG Insights' 2025 analysis found wishlists correlate about 70% with first-month sales (R² 0.49). Only 9% of 1,500 games sold more copies than their launch wishlist count, and 66% of projects had under 10,000 wishlists ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/video-game-insights-steam-wishlists)). Median first-week conversion rises from about 15% under 5k wishlists to about 25% above 100k (Carless data, via [presskit.gg](https://presskit.gg/field-guides/how-many-wishlists-to-launch)). Gamalytic puts month-one conversion at about 30% for full releases and about 20% for Early Access ([Steam Page Analyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/median-steam-game-2026)). The planning bands, all secondary-sourced from Zukowski, are about **7k wishlists for Popular Upcoming**, 30–50k for a $250K+ "Gold" game, and 15–30k as a target for an experienced solo developer in a strong genre. presskit.gg's worked example shows that at $20 and 20% conversion, about 20k launch wishlists support roughly $228K gross in year one ([presskit.gg](https://presskit.gg/field-guides/how-many-wishlists-to-launch)). Timing is as important as volume. Top projects open their page 6–12 months before release and often collect over 70% of wishlists in the final four months ([GameDev Reports](https://gamedevreports.substack.com/p/video-game-insights-steam-wishlists)).

Genre is the tailwind. Zukowski calls genre "the biggest marketing decision" and lists management and simulation among consistent performers over three years ([Unity blog, 2025](https://unity.com/blog/tips-strategies-for-marketing-indie-games)). His 2025 hit rates (share of releases reaching 1,000+ reviews) are 4.1% for simulation and 3.4% for management, against 0.18% for 2D platformers ([presskit.gg](https://presskit.gg/field-guides/how-many-wishlists-to-launch)). Sim audiences review less per sale: estimated multipliers are 20–30× for strategy and sim versus about 30× overall, an estimate rather than a measurement ([Steam Page Analyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/boxleiter-method-explained)). Sovereign's reviews will therefore understate its sales.

Next Fest is a one-shot weapon. Editions have swollen from 800–1,000 demos in 2023 to 2,300–3,000 since late 2024. The median entrant gains about 18 followers (under 500 wishlists), the top 10% about 3,000 and the top 5% about 7,000, and breakouts without prior momentum are rare ([GameDiscoverCo, Oct 2025](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/who-won-october-2025s-steam-next)). Valve allows **one Next Fest per game, ever**. The game must be unreleased with a public page and a release date after the Fest. The demo is a separate app that must pass build review about three weeks before the Fest ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/upcoming_events/nextfest)). The February 2027 edition runs **Feb 22 – Mar 1, 2027, with registration closing Jan 10, 2027** ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/upcoming_events/nextfest/feb_2027)). With Gate A still pending and no public page, Sovereign should skip it. Entering without a polished demo or banked wishlists would spend its only Fest at the median. The rational target is a later edition (June 2027 or after; those dates were not retrieved) about 2–4 months before launch. Before that, the sequence is to release the demo weeks early, press "email wishlisters" within two weeks of the demo launching, and vet the demo with creators ([Unity blog](https://unity.com/blog/tips-strategies-for-marketing-indie-games)). Steam Playtest is the right pre-Fest testing vehicle. It does not affect wishlists, keys can go out before the store page exists, and Valve advises against running it during Next Fest ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/features/playtest)).

Price, Early Access and publishers each carry a clear signal. The median price of top-50 new games by revenue fell from $23.70 to $20.35 between 2023 and 2025 ([Steam Page Analyzer citing GameDiscoverCo](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/median-steam-game-2026)), so $19.99–24.99 is market-standard for a deep sim. Cliff Harris priced Democracy 3 at $25 and argued for "price for quality, not competition" ([Positech, 2013](https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2013/11/19/lessons-from-launching-democracy-3/)). Valve caps launch discounts at 10–40% for 7–14 days, set before release, and enforces 30-day cooldowns. Wishlisters get an email at launch regardless, and a discount email only at 20% off or more ([Steamworks Discounts](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/discounts); [Visibility](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/visibility)). Early Access is a single launch, not two. In 2026 only 21% of graduates earned more in their 1.0 month than in their first EA month, after a median 15 months in EA, though strategy is overrepresented among the exceptions ([GameDiscoverCo, May 2026](https://newsletter.gamediscover.co/p/what-happened-to-early-access-graduates)). On publishers, strategy specialist Hooded Horse guaranteed at least $100K of marketing per game in 2023 ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/marketing/how-hooded-horse-seeks-to-do-right-by-developers)). Yet its CEO said in January 2026 that most indies should self-publish rather than sign with typical publishers, which he called "predators" ([Game World Observer](https://gameworldobserver.com/2026/01/28/hooded-horse-indie-developers-with-access-to-analytics-sell-their-games-better-than-95-of-indie-publishers)). Revenue-split terms are not public. Creator outreach works through opt-in platforms rather than cold email. Plan for about 100 direct contacts plus a platform campaign, start launch outreach three weeks out, and use Curator Connect for up to 100 curators ([Steam Page Analyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/how-to-get-streamers-to-play-your-game)).

## Political and deep-sim makers converged on one template

**Positech (Democracy)** is the closest analogue. Cliff Harris's UK micro-studio coded Democracy 4's engine with two programmers and bought art, SFX, music and localization from third parties. Democracy 4 spent about 15 months in Early Access, from October 2020 to January 2022 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy_(video_game))). Harris's Democracy 3 lessons were these: a voiced trailer helped, pre-release paid ads were his most successful marketing experiment, a 10% launch discount was just "losing 10% of revenue", and Workshop support should be built in from day one ([Positech, 2013](https://www.positech.co.uk/cliffsblog/2013/11/19/lessons-from-launching-democracy-3/)). The cautionary part is directly relevant to Sovereign. From 2018, Facebook repeatedly blocked Democracy ads as political advertising, each dispute took days, and Harris moved "tens of thousands of dollars" of spend to other platforms ([TechRaptor, 2018](https://techraptor.net/gaming/news/democracy-devs-facebook-ads-blocked-as-political-ads)). Critics also argued with the model itself, calling its death-penalty, drug and labour assumptions inaccurate. Meanwhile The Guardian used Democracy 4 to run UK party manifestos ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Democracy_(video_game))). A policy model on real geography becomes a public argument.

The fictional-proxy route is the main alternative. Suzerain's small Berlin team set its politics in a fictional Cold War-style state and framed it as "multi-perspective political entertainment". The game reached 1.4M players and attracted a Krafton-led seed round in November 2025 ([GamesBeat](https://gamesbeat.com/torpor-games-raises-funding-to-expand-suzerain-universe-and-make-the-conformist/)). Its business grew over five years through Switch, mobile, console and DLC releases ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzerain_(video_game))). Papers, Please used the fictional Arstotzka and sold 5M copies in a decade, with steady ports ([Game Developer, 2023](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/papers-please-has-sold-5-million-copies-in-a-decade)). Sovereign's real-geography choice is defensible, but it needs a published neutrality and modeling-transparency policy, data-driven formulas players can inspect and mod, and possibly an alternate-history or abstracted political layer on top of real maps.

The deep-sim cases set realistic expectations for time and money. Shadow Empire took one designer 4–5 years and shipped through Slitherine ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shadow_Empire)). Ostriv's developer started alone in 2014, kept code and design himself ("code is the design document"), delegated art via Trello, and remains in alpha in 2026. He refuses to promise dates, has an estimated ~210k copies sold, and says cosmetic DLC fully repaid its time ([Mezha](https://mezha.ua/en/articles/ostriv-interview-314775/)). Workers & Resources spent about five years in Early Access. After 1.0 it sold "World Maps" DLC based on real regions and an "Early Start" 1920s–50s era DLC ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers_%26_Resources:_Soviet_Republic)), which is a ready-made monetization path for Sovereign's geography and era features. RimWorld's 2–3 person team ran about five years of alpha/EA, passed 1M sales in February 2018 ([Ludeon](https://ludeon.com/blog/2018/01/one-million-copies-sold/)), and has shipped five paid expansions with Workshop modding at its core. It was also briefly pulled from Steam in Australia over a classification refusal in 2022 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RimWorld)). Dwarf Fortress's $30 Steam release sold 300k+ copies in a week, yet Tarn Adams said the money, spread over 20 years, amounts to roughly a normal tech salary ([Game World Observer](https://gameworldobserver.com/2022/12/13/dwarf-fortress-300k-units-sold-zach-tarn-adams-millionaires)). Across all of them, success came from depth plus years of community-facing updates, not speed. That is good news for a part-time developer only if the pace is sustainable. Ostriv's creator credits a slow daily rhythm with no crunch, and he also admits to past creative crises and long stagnation ([Mezha](https://mezha.ua/en/articles/ostriv-interview-314775/)).

## A near-zero tool stack covers most of the pipeline

Most supporting infrastructure is free at solo scale. Codecks' free tier covers up to five users and three projects ([Codecks](https://manual.codecks.io/plans)). Sentry's free tier allows 5k errors a month and BugSplat's 15k crashes. Paid tiers start around $26–37 a month, per a 2026 comparison by a competing vendor that should be checked against list pages ([Bugnet](https://bugnet.io/blog/how-much-does-crash-reporting-cost-for-indie-games)). Crowdin's free plan hosts 60k words, enough for a small game ([costbench](https://costbench.com/compare/crowdin-vs-lokalise/)). SteamDB's release statistics are free ([SteamDB](https://steamdb.info/stats/releases/)). Add GitHub Actions with steamcmd for builds, Steam Playtest plus Discord for testing, and a static press page. The paid lines are the $100 app fee, art, music, trailer and translation. Crash and analytics SDKs collect personal data such as IP addresses, so EU sales call for opt-in consent and a privacy policy. That is general practice rather than a sourced finding here.

Templates are best taken from primary sources rather than from GDD boilerplate. Useful ones include Pfeifer's connected-systems playable criteria, the milestone exit checklist above, Valve's Deck compatibility checks, the GAG basic tier ([GAG](https://gameaccessibilityguidelines.com/full-list/)) and the 23 Xbox Accessibility Guidelines ([Xbox](https://devdocs.xbox.com/build/game-principles/accessibility/xbox-accessibility-guidelines)), Valve's Next Fest workback (−5 weeks for preview submission, −3 weeks for demo build review, −11 days for press preview) ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/marketing/upcoming_events/nextfest)), and Zukowski's pre-page checklist. That checklist says to finalize genre, pick a visual style, show at least three distinct locations, hire a capsule artist and cut a ~30-second gameplay trailer ([WN Hub](https://wnhub.io/news/stores-and-publishing/item-47311)).

The AI assistant affects compliance in one specific way. Valve's rewritten content survey (about January 2026) asks only about AI content that ships and is consumed by players, or that is generated at runtime with guardrails described. "Efficiency gains", such as code assistance, fall outside it ([Steamworks](https://partner.steamgames.com/doc/gettingstarted/contentsurvey); [GIGAZINE](https://gigazine.net/gsc_news/en/20260120-steam-updates-ai-disclosure-guidelines)). AI-generated art, text or capsules in Sovereign would need disclosure, so an asset provenance log should start now. Sentiment matters too: 52% of developers surveyed at GDC 2026 said generative AI is hurting the industry ([GDC](https://gdconf.com/article/gdc-2026-state-of-the-game-industry-reveals-impact-of-layoffs-generative-ai-and-more/)), and players skew similarly. A visible disclosure on a premium page carries reputational cost.

## Most solo games fail at discovery, not at code

The base rates are harsh. Steam had about 21.3k releases in 2025 and 16,115 by August 20, 2026, about 70 a day ([Notebookcheck](https://www.notebookcheck.net/Steam-averages-almost-70-new-games-a-day-in-2026.1374113.0.html)). 48.8% of 2025 releases had 10 or fewer reviews in December ([GamingOnLinux](https://www.gamingonlinux.com/2025/12/over-19000-games-have-released-on-steam-in-2025-with-nearly-half-seeing-fewer-than-10-reviews/page=1/)), and 40% did not recoup the $100 fee ([WN Hub](https://wnhub.io/news/stores-and-publishing/item-49120)). The right comparison set is narrower, though. Gamalytic's 2023 estimates put the median gross at about $700 for all paid games, but about $17,000 for games priced $10 or more and about $8k for 4X and colony sims. It also classed over 70% of releases as hobby projects ([WN Hub](https://wnhub.io/news/stores-and-publishing/item-42136)). A premium, well-marketed political sim is not playing the median game's odds. It still sits in a distribution where the 90th percentile of lifetime gross is about $198K ([Steam Page Analyzer](https://www.steampageanalyzer.com/blog/median-steam-game-2026)).

The documented failure modes map onto Sovereign as follows. **Vision churn** is the Game Outcomes Project's top negative ([Skarupke](https://probablydance.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/10/evidence_for_how_to_make_great_games.pdf)); freeze the one-page hook at Gate A. **Scope creep** came through in Cordingley's three modes, when players wanted only one, and in his advice to "start with the smallest viable idea, then cut it further" ([Game Developer](https://www.gamedeveloper.com/business/concealed-intent-development-postmortem)); era choice and real geography are a combinatorial content multiplier, so ship one era and a bounded map at 1.0 and sell the rest as W&R-style DLC. **Late marketing** shows up as the median Next Fest result and the 70%-in-the-last-four-months curve; the page must go up long before the game feels ready. **Platform risk** includes Meta's political-ad blocks, classification refusals and a DMCA takedown that pulled W&R from Steam in 2023 ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workers_%26_Resources:_Soviet_Republic)). **Burnout** follows from crunch, which correlated negatively even with timeliness ([Game Outcomes](https://gamedeveloper.com/business/the-game-outcomes-project-part-3-game-development-factors)) and is the obvious failure for someone with a full-time job. Missing tutorials were the recurring Early Access complaint against W&R, and complexity forced Cordingley's simplification. Onboarding is therefore a design deliverable, not polish.

## Conclusion

The research reframes Sovereign's next year. The binding constraint is not engineering but **calendar coupling**: a one-time Next Fest, a store page that needs months of compounding, Valve verification and the 30-day first-title wait, and a wishlist curve that bunches in the final four months. Together these mean marketing work has to start before the game feels ready, which is exactly when solo developers resist it. The practical move is to treat Gate C (marketing slice plus public store page) as a go/no-go market test. Below a few thousand wishlists after several months of beats, the hook or the genre framing needs rework before more production hours go in. Pass the February 2027 Fest and plan for a later one.

The second insight is that Sovereign's distinctive features are both its moat and its risk. A deterministic core, real geography and era choice are what Positech, W&R and RimWorld turned into decade-long tails through moddable data, region and era DLC, and transparent models. The same features invite accuracy disputes, ad-platform blocks and combinatorial scope. A part-time solo studio wins this genre the way its predecessors did: ship a narrow, deep 1.0 at a confident premium price, make the model inspectable and moddable, and let the long tail carry the business. Racing a launch spike is the losing strategy.
